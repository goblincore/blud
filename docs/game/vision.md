# GOBLIN — Game Vision (draft 3)

**Status:** draft 3, 2026-09-10. A clean write-up of the general shape agreed
in conversation. Details are still open (§14), but the structure is settled.
**Production scope:** [production-scope.md](production-scope.md).

**Working title:** *GOBLIN*. Final title parked (§14).

**What this doc is:** not a story bible. It's a set of *laws* that produce a
mood, the way Quake's brown stone, runes and silence produce Quake. When a
content decision is unclear, the pillars and world laws settle it.

---

## 1. The game in one paragraph

A goblin lives in a small basement flat it can never leave. Upstairs, a party
has been going on forever; the kick drum comes through the ceiling. The goblin
plays a shareware shooter on a beige CRT: ten levels, a tour of every classic
FPS location, where bodies give like wet clay under fire. That shooter is the
real game. Around it are the room, the desktop, mail from nobody, and a knock
at the door. Things come out of the screen, still wet. The frame starts gentle,
turns strange, and ends sinister. Nothing is explained. It's dark, grotesque
and played completely straight, which is exactly why it's funny.

**Pitch line:** *The game from the back of the box that never existed.*
**Tagline candidate:** *You weren't invited.*

**Referencing rule:** reference *types* of level, enemy and moment, never
specific maps, names, layouts or iconography.

## 3. Pillars

Each pillar has a **therefore** (what it forces) and an **anti-goal** (what it forbids).

**P1 — The world does not explain itself.**
Therefore: no cutscenes, dialogue, codex or narrator; meaning lives in names,
objects, placement and short text on screens. Anti-goal: a lore wiki.

**P2 — Flesh gives.** The SDF deformation is the heart of the game.
Therefore: enemies and bosses are designed from how their bodies deform and
come apart; weapons are judged by what they do to a body. Anti-goal:
hitscan-and-fall-over enemies, bodies that read as hard.

**P3 — Game places inside, one real place outside.**
Therefore: FPS levels are places *from games*, not from life, each with
something that doesn't work. The flat is the opposite: small, specific,
lived-in. Anti-goal: realistic simulacra in the FPS; a generic room outside.

**P4 — Played straight, staged cheap (the B-movie rule).**
Therefore: humour lives in names, objects and situations; the world is deadly
serious. Anti-goal: quips, winking, meme humour. The goblin never talks; it
grunts, giggles and wheezes.

**P5 — Sound is architecture.**
Therefore: music is placed *in* the world, in both layers, and fights, bosses
and the knock follow its rhythm. Anti-goal: a looping combat track pasted over
a level.

**P6 — The shooter is the product; the frame is where the strange lives.**
Therefore: the FPS is complete and great on its own. The frame is seasoning.
Anti-goal: an inner game that's a prop, or a frame that makes shooting feel
pointless.

**P7 — Gore inside, body horror outside.**
Therefore: the FPS gets gore (wounds, gibs, bone). The flat gets body horror:
transformation, softness, wetness, slow and intimate. Anti-goal: gore in the
flat, except the one moment that earns it (§9, ending 1).

## 4. World laws

1. **There is always a party upstairs.** Above the flat, and above you in every
   level. Its music never stops.
2. **Everything is flesh eventually.** In the FPS, under violence. Outside it,
   just with time: the computer, the screen, the door.
3. **Nobody speaks.** Text exists only on screens and paper.
4. **Game places are arranged, not built.** Things look set out for someone
   absent: plates laid, seats in rows, hooks waiting.
5. **The goblin is always smaller than the room.** Except in the flat.
6. **What comes out, stays.** Things from the FPS arrive in the flat and remain.
7. **The layers leak more as the game goes on.**
8. **Space doesn't give.** Everything else in the game is soft sooner or later. The
   stars are the one clean, cold, beautiful thing, and the train burns flesh to
   reach them (§10.3). Only the Party can spoil them.

## 5. The Never list

Adding to this list is cheap. Removing from it needs a real reason.

- Why the goblin can't leave the flat.
- What the Host is, whether it was ever a person, why it throws the Party.
- Whether the Party in the game and the party upstairs are the same.
- Who knocks, who sends the mail, how they know.
- Whether the Host's heartbeat and the knock are the same thing.
- Whether anything can go *into* the screen.
- The goblin's face. At most: hands, a shadow, a covered mirror.
- Who runs the Line, where it started, and how a train flies.
- What the Stoker is, and what it is feeding the fire with (you can see; it's never said).

## 6. Structure: three layers

| Layer | What it is | Camera |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **The Flat** | A small basement flat. Fully explorable, decorated over time, **never leavable** | First person, walk and look |
| **The Desktop** | A made-up 90s OS on the beige CRT: the FPS, mail, files, a music player | Leaning into the monitor |
| **The FPS** | The shareware shooter: ~10 levels, stops on a train line that runs up into space (§10.3) | Full screen, bezel almost gone |

**Moving between layers is a camera move, never a menu.** Sitting down leans
into the monitor; launching the game pushes in until the bezel is a faint edge
(an option removes it). **Escape pulls back** a layer. The pause menu *is*
pulling back.

### 6.1 Pacing

**First launch, the cold open.** No title screen. The game starts inside the
FPS on *The Wake*, the demo level (5–8 minutes). Its last pickup is a CD in an
open coffin. Picking it up starts one continuous pull-back: the bezel appears
for the first time, then the desk, then the room. The screen bulges and the CD
pushes out, **gooey and wet**, onto the desk. Then nothing prompts you.

**After that it goes quiet.** The gentle phase is spent half-doubting that
happened; the stain on the bezel is the only proof. The screen doesn't give
again until the strange phase.

**Every later launch boots into the Desktop.** The computer's startup *is* the
title screen: CRT warm-up, memory count, chime. Continue and settings are
desktop icons. Only New Game uses a plain menu, and it leads to the cold open.

### 6.2 Tone phases

The frame shifts in three phases, driven by FPS progress. Dread stays quiet;
never jump scares.

| Phase | Levels | Flat | Desktop and boot | Portal |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Gentle** | 1–2 | Cosy clutter, warm lamp; the fan almost sounds like breathing | Friendly spam, tip of the day | CD tray, printer: dry, mechanical, funny |
| **Strange** | 3–5 | Objects shift, routines change, the computer case has pores | Mail about what you just did; files you didn't make; the memory count is off | Parcels through the mail slot; things come out warm; the screen gives again |
| **Sinister** | 6–8 | The ceiling bulges softly; the door gives under the hand | Mail about what you *haven't* done; a boot that breathes | The screen gives routinely; things come through wet |

## 7. The Flat

- **Small and specific:** one main room (maybe a tiny bathroom): desk and CRT,
  a chair too big for a goblin, a mattress, the CD shelf, pipes that carry the
  music, a bucket under a leak, a high window showing only passing feet, a
  covered mirror, and the front door.
- **The door never lets you out,** and refuses differently per phase: the
  goblin turns back as if it forgot why it got up; the door opens onto the same
  room; the door is soft.
- **Decorating:** no currency, no shop. Objects arrive as consequences of play
  (§8.1) and can be placed anywhere, doll's-house style. Decorations are saved
  and matter in the ending.
- **Routines** (*UFO*): footsteps at set times, pipes knocking, the Party's set
  changing on the hour. Paying attention unlocks things.

## 8. What crosses over

One loop ties the layers together:
**find it in the FPS → it comes through → it goes on the shelf → you use it to
answer the knock → it shapes the ending.**

### 8.1 The portal

Things arrive in the flat through a route that escalates with the phases
(§6.2): **CD tray and printer → mail slot → the screen itself.** The screen
push-through is the signature image: glass creaking in a way glass can't, a wet
stretch, a slap on the desk, the membrane settling slowly, and a film on the
glass that stays. It uses the same deformation as the guests, which is why it
reads as wrong.

**The beige machine becomes a body** on the same schedule: a fan that breathes,
a case with pores, a tray that comes out like a tongue, a bezel that sweats.
The door changes alongside it.

### 8.2 Music you collect

The main collectible is **music**: one CD per level plus extras, credited to
made-up artists and labels, each with a prerendered CG cover and deadpan liner
notes.

- **Found** in the FPS as secrets (a disc on a pedestal, a guest's Walkman, a DJ
  booth). You hear it in the level before you find it.
- **Delivered** through the portal; MP3s also arrive by modem or mail.
- **Shelved** as the room's main decoration, **played** on the stereo or
  desktop player, **burned** into mixes that can go under the door.

### 8.3 The knock

Someone knocks, throughout the game. You can't open the door, but you can
**answer**: knock back, stay silent, play a track, slide something under. The
knock is a rhythm taken from the soundtrack, so **the right track is the right
reply**. It becomes a wordless language (*L.O.L.*, prisoners tapping on a wall):
friendly, then insistent, then knocking from places a door isn't. It's the
goblin's only relationship.

## 9. Endings (candidates)

1. **You built the last level.** The Party comes downstairs. The final fight is
   in **your flat**: the HUD appears over the room, guests pour through the
   door, every shelf and rug you placed is cover, and **the soundtrack is your
   CD shelf in order**. The one time gore enters the flat. *Favourite.*
2. **The knock from the glass.** The last knock comes from inside the monitor,
   in your own knock pattern. All game you couldn't get out; this is about what
   gets in.
3. **The right answer.** If you learned the language, the door opens by itself
   onto an empty, lit stairwell. The goblin looks at it, then sits back down.
   Credits roll over the FPS title screen.

**Likely shape:** 1 as the climax, then 2 or 3 as the epilogue, depending on
how you answered the knock across the game.

## 10. The FPS: *the shareware episode*

### 10.1 Scope

**About 10 levels, the size of a classic first episode.** One person can make
this well. Finishing it shows an *"Order the full version!"* nag screen for
floors that don't exist; the order form goes under the door. Whether anything
comes back is open (§14).

### 10.2 The goblin

Grotesque first, funny second, never cool. First-person hands are the
character: dirty nails, knuckle hair, rings stolen from guests.

| Verb | Consequence |
| --- | --- |
| **Scurry** | Fast, low, twitchy; low eye height |
| **Squeeze** | Vents, gaps, dumbwaiters: goblin-only routes and secrets |
| **Scavenge** | Weapons are junk stolen from guests |
| **Giggle** | The only voice: giggles on kills, wheezes when hurt |

### 10.3 The Line (hub) — decided 2026-09-23

**The train is the hub.** After the Wake, a train line runs past the back of the
cemetery. You board in level 1, *Night Train*, fight carriage by carriage to the
engine, and at the end the train leaves the rails and climbs into space. From
then on, **the space between levels is the train**, and every level is a stop on
the Line. The last stop is the terminus: the Party, which is Hell.

This keeps the game abstract (the track is the only explanation for why each
door opens onto a different world), keeps the hub linear and cheap to build (a
chain of carriages that grows as you progress), and follows world law 1:
height is closeness to the Party, and space is as far up as it goes.

**The carriages (the hub proper).** Quiet between stops. Each finished level
leaves something behind in the carriages, so the train fills up as the flat does.
- **The coat-check carriage** is the old Cloakroom, moved on board: rails of
  coats that may be skins, a counter, a bell nobody answers. Claim tickets are
  redeemed here; some only work outside the game.
- **A departure board** is the only text: the next stop's name, within the word
  budget. No announcements, no conductor speaking.
- **Windows show what can't be there:** your flat, a level you haven't reached,
  the Party lit up far overhead, getting closer.

**The Stoker (proposal).** The engine has no driver. It has a Stoker: a huge,
soft figure in the cab, back always to you, shovelling into the firebox without
stopping. **The fire runs on bodies.** You can see what goes in; the game never
says. The goblin starts the game with a gravedigger's shovel; the Stoker is the
same verb at the scale of the whole journey. It never turns round, never speaks,
never fights. Between stops you can walk to the cab and watch it work. It is the
one character who is always with you.
- **Why it works:** it puts the game's thesis in one image: *the trip to the
  stars is powered by flesh.* Aspiration fuelled by bodies, played straight.
- **Alternatives if it doesn't land:** *the Conductor*, a thin figure who punches
  your claim ticket at every carriage door (it becomes the ticket system); or
  *the empty cab*, which drives itself and has a seat exactly goblin-sized.

**Space.** Romantic, aspirational, and the opposite of everything else in the
game, and that opposition is the point (world law 8).
- **Stars are clean.** Deep blue-black, sharp points, slow nebulae, the prerender
  look at its most beautiful. Nothing soft out there.
- **The one wrong light:** from the lift-off on, a warm pink smear sits in one
  window, far away. Each stop it is a little bigger. It's the Party, the tumour,
  the terminus. By the last carriage it fills the glass.
- **The Stoker's fire** is the only warm light in the cab, and the only thing on
  the train that looks like the flesh outside the windows ahead.

### 10.4 Levels: a tour of classic FPS locations

As if the fictional developer loved Doom, Quake and Blood too. The variety is
the theme. Every level follows the same rules (§10.6).

| # | Level | Type | What doesn't work | SDF showcase | Enemy spotlight | Phase |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 0 | ***The Wake*** (demo) | Cemetery, funeral home | A wake with nobody left to mourn; a CD in an open coffin; the rail line runs past the back gate | First bodies, first gore | Zombies | Cold open |
| 1 | ***Night Train*** | Train | A party carriage on a line with no stations; the engine at the front, the Stoker at the fire | Motion: carriages still, world moves; **the lift-off into space** | Passengers | Gentle |
| 2 | ***The Keep*** | Gothic castle | A banquet for guests who never come | Stone that goes soft when shot | Gargoyles | Gentle |
| 3 | ***Cold Storage*** | Research lab | Storage that keeps nothing; specimens keep themselves | Glass tanks | Soldiers, a bloatmaw in a tank | Strange |
| 4 | ***The Big Top*** | Circus | A show for empty seats | Rubbery, inflatable, pops | Clowns | Strange |
| 5 | ***The Ship*** | Alien sci-fi | Arranged for a crew shaped like no one | Impossible smooth geometry, low gravity | Cyclops | Strange |
| 6 | ***The Works*** | Factory | Assembly lines making soft flesh dolls for no buyer | Half-assembled bodies | Gnashers, dolls | Sinister |
| 7 | ***Floorfiller*** | Club | A dance floor that swells on the kick | Architecture pulsing to the beat | The crowd; **boss: the Headliner** | Sinister |
| 8 | ***Hell*** (the terminus) | Hell | *Hell is upstairs*: the last stop is the tumour the pink light always was | **The whole level is flesh** | **Boss: the Host** | Sinister |
| S | ***Dollhouse*** (secret, from The Works) | — | The goblin's own flat at giant scale | Room furniture, huge | The mouse | — |
| F | **Your flat** | — | The room you decorated | Gore in the flat, once | The Party | Finale |

**Hell is a tumour.** No lava, no pentagrams. A monstrous growth has swallowed
the top of the building: corridors through tissue, sphincter doors, pieces of
every earlier world half-digested inside it. It pulses to the Party's kick.

**Threads:** The Works leads to Dollhouse (from making dolls to being one).
The Wake is a party for the dead; the finale is a party that comes to you.
Hell and the flat go soft on the same schedule.

### 10.5 Weapons, enemies, bosses

**Weapons.** In the game today: the sawn-off double (*The Short Answer*). Also
modelled or prototyped outside the game: a grapeshot hand-cannon (*Loose
Change*) and the dynamite bundle (*Party Favours*, lab page only). A 10-level
episode needs roughly 5–6 weapons; each must pass the test *what does it do to
a body that nothing else does?*

- **Starting melee** (The Wake): a gravedigger's tool. Candidates: shovel,
  pickaxe, axe. Decision open; see [the Wake design](levels/00-the-wake/design.md).
- **A chainsaw, of course (decided 2026-09-10).** Continuous cutting through
  soft bodies is a deformation showcase no other weapon gives. Found mid-episode.

**Enemies are the guests and staff of the Party:** zombies are spent guests,
soldiers are door security, clowns are the entertainment, gnashers are
bouncers, the bloatmaw is the appetite. Unplaced characters (bonewalker,
minotaur, dragon, schoolgirl) must earn a role or be cut.

**Bosses, only two:**
- **Rules:** the body is the health bar (no bar on screen); no invulnerable
  phases, no sponges, a few minutes long; attacks land on the beat and the
  breakdown is the opening; the arena reacts.
- **The Headliner** (the renamed cyberdemon), on stage in *Floorfiller*. The
  fight follows the set, and its body comes apart in time. When it dies, the
  club's music cuts out for the first time.
- **The Host**, whose body *is* Hell. A heart at its centre pulses to the
  Party's kick; you carve through it while the tumour closes corridors and
  grows over cover. No face. Its heartbeat has the knock's rhythm (never said).

### 10.6 Level rules

1. A place from games, not from life.
2. Something that doesn't work.
3. A party somewhere overhead.
4. One enemy whose deformation is the level's centrepiece.
5. One CD, heard before it's found.
6. Big architecture, small goblin routes.
7. A music source placed in the geometry.
8. Later levels borrow objects from the flat.
9. **One set piece the player triggers** (decided 2026-09-10). Not something
   that happens *to* you: you shoot, pull or break something, and the level
   answers. Ideally you choose the moment, and it follows the music.

**Set-piece candidates** (first pass; only the Wake's is designed):

| Level | Set piece |
| --- | --- |
| The Wake | **The funeral bell:** each toll wakes a wave of the dead ([design](levels/00-the-wake/design.md)) |
| The Keep | The banquet bell or gong: the absent guests arrive to eat |
| Cold Storage | Shoot the freezer controls: every tank thaws at once |
| The Big Top | Cut the ropes: the tent comes down soft over the crowd |
| Night Train | **Feed the fire:** at the engine, blow the firebox open (or stuff it) and the boiler roars; the train leaves the rails and climbs into space. (The emergency brake is a smaller mid-level beat: everything in the carriages flies forward.) |
| The Ship | Vent the airlock: low gravity becomes no gravity, bodies drift |
| The Works | Start the line: the dolls come off the belt finished, and alive |
| Floorfiller | Shoot the speaker stack: the drop hits, the room swells, the Headliner arrives |
| Hell | Cut the first artery: the tumour wakes up |

## 11. Sound and music

**The soundtrack is its own thing:** noise, dungeon synth, trap and hard gabber
combined, released under made-up artists.

| Layer | Job |
| --- | --- |
| **Dungeon synth** | The train between stops, exploration, the lonely goblin |
| **Noise** | Dread, transitions, damage, walls breathing |
| **Trap** | Weight and swagger: big guests, security |
| **Hard gabber** | Combat peaks, the club, bosses |

- **Diegetic bleed in both layers:** through the flat's ceiling, through level
  walls. Distance and height decide the muffling.
- **Pulling back is a mix:** the game shrinks to the CRT's small speaker as the
  room's sound rises.
- **The flat's soundscape:** CRT whine, pipes, the leak, footsteps, the knock.
- **Silence is a weapon.** The music stopping, in either layer, is the scariest
  event in the game.
- **Body sounds are half the gun feel:** wet, specific, B-movie foley.

## 12. Visual target

- **The FPS: the magazine prerender.** Glossy flesh, soft light, slightly
  too-clean CG, single dramatic lights, fog. Tungsten orange, sodium, sick
  green, bruise purple, flesh pink, crushed black.
- **The Desktop: its own 90s OS.** Beige, bevels, pixel fonts, dithered icons.
  Not Windows.
- **The Flat: warm, cheap, real.** One lamp, CRT glow, grey window light, until
  it goes soft.
- **VHS and interlacing** belong to the CRT and to moments where layers leak.

## 13. Language budget

| Where | Limit |
| --- | --- |
| Level names | Short; ≤ 3 words |
| Intermission cards | ≤ 60 words, a few per episode |
| In-level signs | ≤ 5 words |
| Mail | ≤ 40 words per message, ≤ 3 per level, no sender names |
| Notes under the door | ≤ 12 words |
| CD liner notes | ≤ 50 words; allowed to be the funniest text in the game |

**Samples.** Intermission card:

> The dishes are done. Nobody thanked you.
> Above, the music changes. Someone has noticed the kitchen is quiet.
> They are putting your name on a list.

Mail, one per phase: *"remember to take breaks. drink water. the pipes are
supposed to make that noise."* · *"you left one of the plates on the floor. we
put it back."* · *"we saved you a seat. it's by the rug."*

Back of the inner game's box: *"THEY DIDN'T INVITE YOU. Revolutionary
SOFTBODY™ technology means no two kills are ever the same! Contains scenes of
extreme gore, loud music and poor manners."*

## 14. Open questions

1. **Ending combination** (§9) and whether the full-version order ties into it.
2. **Title.** *Gob* is taken three times by goblin games, *Goon* is dominated
   by slang, *Gloom* is heavily taken (including a 1995 Amiga FPS). Threads:
   foreign words (*Gobelin*, *Rausch*, *Gaki*, *Duende*), or a separate campy
   name for the inner shareware game.
3. **Knock language:** its first "word", and how many releases the music
   collection has.
4. **Weapons:** the rest of the arsenal.
5. **Roster:** final roles or cuts for unplaced characters; rename the cyberdemon.
6. **Lab vs. alien ship:** distinct enough?
7. **Level count:** 9 main + secret + finale may still be one or two too many.
8. **Flat layout:** one room, or one room plus a tiny bathroom?
9. **The Stoker:** keep it, or the Conductor / the empty cab (§10.3)?
10. **Boarding:** does the cold-open demo end on the CD (then the full game
    resumes at the cemetery's back gate by the tracks), or does the demo include
    the first glimpse of the train?
11. **Between stops:** is the train hub free to walk at any time, or only
    between levels? Can you ride back to an earlier stop?
